Pluto

Pluto was counted as the ninth planet until 2006, when astronomers reclassified it as a dwarf planet. It is a small, icy world in the Kuiper Belt, a region of frozen bodies beyond Neptune.

Clyde Tombaugh discovered Pluto in 1930. It is smaller than Earth's Moon, but NASA's New Horizons flyby in 2015 revealed a surprisingly active world.

Surface and Atmosphere

Pluto's surface is a mix of nitrogen, methane, and water ice. A bright, heart-shaped region called Tombaugh Regio includes Sputnik Planitia, a smooth plain of nitrogen ice with no visible craters, which means that surface is geologically young.

Pluto has a thin atmosphere of nitrogen and methane. It grows thicker when Pluto is closer to the Sun and freezes onto the surface as the planet moves away.

Orbit and Moons

Pluto takes 248 Earth years to orbit the Sun, and its tilted, stretched orbit sometimes carries it inside Neptune's path. A day on Pluto lasts about 6.4 Earth days.

Its largest moon, Charon, is about half Pluto's width. The two circle a point in space between them, so some astronomers describe them as a double system. Pluto has five known moons in all.

Exploration

NASA's New Horizons spacecraft launched in 2006 and flew past Pluto on July 14, 2015, sending back the first close-up images of its surface.

New Horizons kept traveling and later flew past a small Kuiper Belt object named Arrokoth in 2019.
